abstract: >-
  Understanding geodemographic distribution across neighbourhoods is essential for identifying socio-spatial
  differentiation in cities. Traditional geodemographic classification studies rely primarily on various
  census and socioeconomic data, offering limited insight into the street-level environments through which
  such differences are experienced and reproduced. In particular, the visual and perceptual dimensions
  of neighbourhoods remain largely absent from existing classification frameworks. This study advances
  geodemographic classification research by introducing street view imagery (SVI) as a novel source for
  capturing latent visual features linked to socio-spatial structure. We propose an automated machine
  learning framework that leverages 556 image-derived variables to predict socio-demographic groups. The
  framework is applied in ten UK cities, where a national model and local instances are developed to assess
  the extent to which visual-demographic relationships are place-dependent or generalizable. Results show
  that SVI has potential for capturing geodemographic patterns and enabling national-scale generalization.
  The local model achieves an accuracy ranging from 0.49 to 0.70, while the national model brings an accuracy
  of 0.62. Finally, cross-city transfer analysis reveals both shared and heterogeneous visual–demographic
  relationships across urban contexts. This study contributes a new street-level observational layer
  that complements traditional data sources and extends the methodological scope of geodemographics, while
  informing improvements to public spaces and supports policy objectives aimed at fostering inclusive
  neighbourhood environments.
summary: >-
  This study introduces street view imagery as a new data source for geodemographic classification. Using
  556 image-derived variables across semantic, object, scene, pixel, and spatial categories, an automated
  machine learning framework predicts UK Output Area Classification groups in ten UK cities. Local models
  reach accuracies of 0.49–0.70 and a national model reaches 0.62, while cross-city transfer analysis
  reveals both shared and city-specific visual–demographic relationships.
